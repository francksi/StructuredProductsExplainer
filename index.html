Structured Products, Explained Simply
Start here — a plain-English guide before diving into specific structures
A structured product is an investment with pre-agreed rules. Those rules determine when you receive income, when you get your capital back, and when you may lose money.

Most structured products combine: a note issued by a bank, an underlying market (an index, share, fund or credit index), a payoff formula, and conditions such as barriers, coupons, caps or early redemption triggers.
The Four Investor Questions
Before buying any structured product, ask these
1. Will I get income?
Is the coupon guaranteed, conditional, or absent? If conditional — on what?

2. When do I get my capital back?
Is there an early redemption trigger? Can I sell before maturity? At what cost?

3. What market level causes a loss?
What is the barrier? Is it European (final close only) or American (any time)? How far is it from current levels?

4. Who am I relying on?
What bank issued the note? What happens if they default? Capital protection is only as good as the issuer's credit.
What can go right
Why investors use structured products
Higher income: Yields typically 2–5× the risk-free rate, in exchange for taking defined risks.

Downside protection: Capital barriers and floors absorb moderate market falls before losses occur.

Defined outcomes: Unlike equities, the possible payoffs are pre-defined — investors can model scenarios before committing.

Access to complex strategies: Twin Win, absolute return, fund participation — payoffs not available from direct investment.
What can go wrong
Risks that every investor must understand
Issuer default: If the bank that issued the note defaults, capital protection and coupons may fail — regardless of the product's terms.

Barrier breach: A 70% barrier does not mean you can only lose 30%. If the index closes below 70% at maturity, you may lose from par — proportionally.

Selling before maturity: Capital protection applies at maturity only. Selling early may result in a loss even if the product would have paid out in full.

Opportunity cost: Every structured product trades something — upside cap, coupon income, or flexibility — for protection or yield.

The Building Blocks
Understand these terms first — they appear in every structured product
Issuer
The bank that creates and guarantees the note
The issuer (e.g. Goldman Sachs, Barclays, BNP Paribas) creates the structured note and is legally obligated to make all payments. All capital protection, coupon payments, and redemption amounts are dependent on the issuer not defaulting. Even a 100% capital protected note carries the credit risk of its issuer. GGC sources notes from 27+ investment-grade issuers (A/A+ rated) to provide access and competitive pricing.
Underlying
The market or asset the product is linked to
The underlying determines the product's payoff. Common underlyings include stock indices (Euro Stoxx 50, S&P 500, Nikkei), individual equities (Nvidia, HSBC, Accenture), funds (JPMorgan Global Select), and credit indices (iTraxx Main). The underlying's performance at specific observation dates drives the income and capital outcome — the investor does not own the underlying directly.
Strike / Initial Level
The reference point — 100% of the starting price
The strike (or initial level) is the price of the underlying on the trade date. It is set at 100% and becomes the reference point for all barriers, caps, and return calculations. Example: if the Euro Stoxx 50 is at 4,800 on trade date, that level = 100%. A barrier at 70% = 3,360. All future payoffs are measured as a percentage of this initial level — not in absolute points.
Maturity
The scheduled end date of the product
The maturity date is when the product expires and final payments are made. Capital protection applies only at maturity — if an investor sells before maturity, the market value of the note may be below par. Most structured products have tenors of 1–5 years, with some autocall structures potentially redeeming earlier. Selling before maturity means accepting the prevailing secondary market price, which may be significantly below the protected level.
Observation Date
The scheduled dates when conditions are checked
Observation dates are the scheduled dates (typically quarterly or annually) when the product checks whether autocall, coupon, or barrier conditions are met. For most structures, only the closing price on an observation date matters — not intraday levels. European barriers are checked only on the final maturity date. American barriers are checked on every observation date (or continuously) — a materially different risk.
Coupon
The income paid by the product
Coupons are either guaranteed (paid regardless of market performance — Reverse Convertibles) or conditional (paid only if the underlying meets a condition on the observation date — Phoenix/Autocall). Conditional coupons may be memory coupons: if a coupon is missed because the underlying was below the coupon barrier, it is stored and paid at the next qualifying date. If the condition is never met again, the stored coupons may remain unpaid.
Autocall Barrier
The level that triggers early redemption
If the underlying closes at or above the autocall barrier on a scheduled observation date, the product redeems early — paying capital plus any accrued coupon. The autocall level is typically 100% of strike (the product redeems if the market is flat or higher) but can be set lower (sub-par autocall). Good news: you get capital back early. Bad news: you stop earning coupons and must reinvest at prevailing — potentially lower — rates.
Coupon Barrier
The level the underlying must be above to earn a coupon
The coupon barrier is a separate threshold from the capital barrier. If the underlying is below the coupon barrier on an observation date, no coupon is paid that period (it may be stored as memory). The coupon barrier is typically set at 60–75% of strike. A product can have a 70% coupon barrier and a 70% capital barrier as separate rules — though they are often set at the same level.
Capital Barrier (KI)
The level that puts your invested capital at risk
The capital barrier (knock-in, or KI) is the level below which the investor's capital is no longer protected. Critical: a 70% barrier does not mean you can only lose 30%. If the underlying finishes at 55% of strike, you receive 55% of capital — a 45% capital loss.

European KI (most common): only the final maturity closing price is checked — intraday or interim lows do not matter.
American KI: any closing price (or continuous trading) below the barrier permanently triggers the knock-in.
Low Strike
The put option strike in Reverse Convertibles — creates geared loss
In a Reverse Convertible, the investor sells a put option struck at the low strike level (typically 75–85% of initial). Unlike a simple capital barrier, the low strike creates a geared loss below the strike: capital returned = (final level ÷ low strike) × 100. Example: index at 60%, low strike 80% → capital = 60/80 × 100 = 75%. The loss accelerates below the strike because of the put option mechanics — not 1-for-1 from par.
Participation & Cap
How much upside the investor receives — and its ceiling
Participation is the percentage of the underlying's gain that the investor receives. 100% = same as the index. 112% = investor earns 12% more than the index gain. A cap is the maximum gain (e.g. +25% for Twin Win, +40.95% for Capital Protected USD). Capped upside is the price of downside protection or enhanced participation — the premium from the sold call funds the floor or the absolute-return feature.
Worst-of
The weakest asset in a basket determines your capital
Worst-of does not mean diversified protection. It means the weakest asset controls the result.

In a worst-of basket of 3 stocks, the capital barrier is measured against the worst-performing stock at maturity — regardless of what the other two do. If one stock falls 50% below its individual strike, your capital is affected even if the other two are up 80%. The higher coupon on worst-of products compensates for this concentration risk.
Issuer Credit Risk
The risk that the bank that issued your note defaults
Every structured note carries issuer credit risk — the risk that the issuing bank defaults before or at maturity. If the issuer defaults, capital protection and coupons may not be paid, regardless of the product's terms. This is distinct from market risk. GGC mitigates this by sourcing from 27+ A/A+ rated banks, but an issuer rating is not a guarantee. Always consider the issuer's creditworthiness as a separate risk factor.
Secondary Market / Liquidity
What happens if you need to sell before maturity
Structured notes can typically be sold before maturity via the issuer's secondary market or a broker. However, the secondary market price reflects current market conditions — it may be significantly below par, especially early in the note's life or during market stress. Capital protection applies at maturity only. Selling early means accepting the prevailing bid price. Investors should treat structured products as held-to-maturity instruments unless they are prepared to absorb secondary market risk.

Autocall
The early redemption mechanism that terminates the product if underlyings perform well
Core Mechanic
How it works
On each observation date (e.g. quarterly), if the worst-performing underlying is at or above the Autocall Barrier (typically 100% of initial), the note is redeemed early and the investor receives 100% of capital (plus any coupon due). The product ceases to exist. If the underlying is below the barrier, the product simply rolls to the next observation.

Investor perspective
Why it's good for the client
The investor receives their capital back faster when markets perform well — getting back capital to redeploy. In exchange for accepting this early exit risk, they receive a higher coupon than a comparable vanilla bond. It turns a 2-year product into something that might resolve in 3–6 months if markets cooperate.
GGC Example — 1Y Phoenix Autocall Memory on Indices (USD) Euro Stoxx 50 / S&P 500 / Nikkei 225 basket. 80% coupon barrier, 70% European capital barrier, 11.70% p.a. memory coupon. Quarterly autocall at 100%. If all three indices are flat or positive at the first quarterly observation, investor receives 100% + 2.925% (one quarter of 11.70%) and exits — capital returned in 3 months instead of waiting 12.
Issuer / Broker perspective
Why it's valuable for the desk
The autocall is essentially a short call option on the underlying sold by the investor. It provides the issuer with the cash to fund the enhanced coupon: the probability-weighted present value of early redemption lets the desk price a higher running yield into the structure. It also caps the issuer's delta hedging complexity — the book doesn't run forever.
Pricing logic If there is a 30% probability the product autocalls at Q1, the issuer only needs to provision hedging capital for ~0.7 of a full-term product. The expected duration shortens, reducing funding cost — and that saving is passed back as coupon.
The Trade-off
Reinvestment risk: the hidden cost to the investor
When the product autocalls early in a strong bull market, the investor misses the remaining coupons AND must find a new product. In a rising rate environment this can be favourable; in a falling rate environment (like 2024-2025) they may reinvest at lower yields. This is the autocall's real risk to the investor — not capital loss, but opportunity cost.
Investor
Gets higher coupon + faster capital return when markets rise. Accepts reinvestment risk.
Issuer
Books a short call, reducing expected duration and hedging complexity. Pocket the premium by offering higher yield.
Broker/Distributor
Autocalling products generate repeat business. Client reinvests → new product → new distribution fee. Aligns incentives.

Because the return derives from an option contract (a form of permissible risk transfer) rather than a fixed interest obligation, autocall structures may be considered Sharia-compliant from a yield-mechanism perspective.

Why option premium ≠ interest: Interest (riba) is a predetermined, contractually guaranteed return on a loan regardless of outcome. An option premium is compensation for bearing a defined market risk — the investor may have to absorb losses if the index falls. This contingent, risk-linked return is the key distinction.

Phoenix & Memory Coupon
Conditional income with a "catch-up" mechanism — the coupon that remembers what it owes you
Core Mechanic
Phoenix = Autocall + Conditional Coupon
A Phoenix adds a Coupon Barrier (e.g. 70–80%) below the Autocall Barrier (100%). On each observation date:

• If worst-of ≥ Autocall Barrier (100%) → Early redemption (product ends)
• If worst-of ≥ Coupon Barrier (e.g. 75%) but < 100% → Coupon is paid, product continues
• If worst-of < Coupon Barrier → No coupon this period (product continues)

The Memory feature means: if a coupon is missed (underlying below barrier), that coupon is remembered and paid in full the next time the coupon condition is triggered.
Why the Memory matters
Missed coupons are deferred — not automatically recovered
Without memory: if a coupon is missed in Q1, it is lost permanently. With memory: the missed Q1 coupon is stored. When the coupon condition is next met (e.g. Q2), you receive both Q1 and Q2 together. Important: if the coupon condition is never met again for the rest of the product's life, the stored coupons remain unpaid. Memory defers — it does not guarantee recovery.

Memory coupon is a powerful client narrative
For a wealth manager presenting to a client: "If markets dip temporarily, you don't lose your income — it catches up." This is much easier to sell than "you may receive no coupon in bad quarters." The memory feature is essentially cheap risk communication: it costs little optionality to add but dramatically improves client psychology and acceptance.
Investor

Capital Barriers
The conditional capital protection — what happens at the bottom
Two Types
European vs American Knock-In
European Knock-In: The barrier is only observed on the final valuation date. Even if the underlying crashes 50% during the product's life, if it recovers above the barrier at maturity, you get 100% capital back. Much cheaper to structure. Better for investors.

American Knock-In (continuous): The barrier is observed every trading day. If the underlying ever touches the barrier — even intraday — capital protection is lost permanently, regardless of where it closes at maturity. More aggressive. Less common in professional client structured notes.
Why lower barriers = higher coupons
The barrier is a sold put option
Every capital protection barrier is the investor selling a put option to the issuer at that strike. A 70% barrier = investor sells a 30% OTM put. A 60% barrier = investor sells a 40% OTM put (cheaper — further OTM). The less protection you keep, the more premium you receive, which flows back as higher coupon.
Example: Phoenix Memory on Euro Stoxx / S&P / Nikkei (USD) 80% Coupon Barrier / 70% Capital Barrier → 11.7% p.a.
70% Coupon Barrier / 60% Capital Barrier → 8.15% p.a.

Moving the capital barrier from 70% to 60% saves 3.55% p.a. — that's the value of the extra 10% put you're selling back.
Issuer: barrier sets the hedging structure
Delta profile and gamma management
The issuer buys a put spread to hedge its barrier obligation. At a 70% barrier, the issuer buys a 100% put and sells a 70% put (a put spread). The barrier defines where the issuer's net delta exposure changes character. This is why the capital barrier must be quoted precisely — it's not aesthetic, it defines the full hedging book.
Worst-case scenario for issuer If the underlying closes exactly at 70.01% (just above barrier), investor gets 100% → issuer must buy back full notional. If it closes at 69.99% (just below), investor gets 69.99%. That 0.02% move creates a binary jump in the issuer's payout → high gamma near barrier at expiry.

Best for income-first investors
The guaranteed coupon is ideal for clients with predictable income needs — family offices, insurance mandates, pension distribution phases. The Low Strike feature
Loss measured from strike, not 100%
The "Low Strike" (e.g. 80%) means the issuer only has to pay out proportional loss below the strike level — not below 100%. This is crucial: if the underlying falls 25%, the standard payout would be 75%. But with an 80% strike, payout = 75%/80% = 93.75%. The strike cushions the investor from the first 20% of decline. The issuer is essentially selling a 20% OTM put financed by the coupon.
Why issuers prefer the Low Strike structure The strike creates a clearly defined liability profile. The issuer knows exactly at what level the note becomes "in the money" for the short put. Combined with the autocall feature, the expected duration shortens, reducing hedging complexity further.

Worst-of Basket
How using multiple underlyings dramatically enhances yield — and risk
Critical concept
Worst-of does not mean diversified protection
It means the weakest asset controls the result. If a basket has 3 stocks and one falls 60% below its strike at maturity, your capital is impaired — even if the other two are up 80%. The higher coupon on worst-of structures compensates for this concentration risk. Before buying a worst-of product, always ask: what is the most plausible scenario in which the weakest stock falls below the barrier?
Core Mechanic
Your return depends on the weakest link
In a worst-of basket, you gain exposure to multiple underlyings simultaneously, but ALL conditions (coupon payment, autocall, capital protection) are determined by the one that performs worst. This is not an average — it is the minimum. The investor earns a premium yield in exchange for accepting that any one stock in the basket can trigger losses or cancel coupons.
Example 2Y One Star Phoenix: Nvidia / Barclays / Accenture — 15.77% p.a. 3 stocks, any one can breach. If Barclays falls 35% while Nvidia is +80% and Accenture is +20%: the product observes Barclays. Coupon may be missed. Capital barrier tested on Barclays alone.
Why worst-of generates higher yield
Correlation is the secret ingredient
The issuer buys a basket of puts (one per underlying) but only has to pay out on one (the worst). The lower the correlation between the stocks, the more valuable this basket of puts is to the issuer — and the higher the coupon they can offer. A basket of Nvidia + Barclays + Accenture is volatile AND has low cross-correlation (tech / banking / consulting). Perfect worst-of candidates.
Correlation arbitrage in practice Single stock (Nvidia alone): implied vol ~70%, structured note might offer 8% p.a.
Worst-of of Nvidia / Barclays / Accenture: correlation ~0.3 → basket worst-of vol much higher → issuer collects much more put premium → 15.77% p.a. offered.
The hidden risk
Correlation can jump in a crisis
Worst-of structures are designed around normal-market correlations. In a systemic crisis (2008, 2020 COVID crash), all assets become highly correlated and fall together. The diversification benefit disappears exactly when you need it most. Investors collecting 15.77% on a 3-stock basket are implicitly selling "crash correlation risk."
Client conversation you must have "This product pays you for the risk that one of three stocks falls significantly. As long as they don't all fall together — which historically they do in a crisis — you are fine. The 15.77% yield IS the price of that tail correlation risk."
One Star: The escape hatch
What makes the "One Star" structure clever
The One Star feature adds a lifeline: at maturity, if ANY ONE underlying is above its initial level, you get 100% capital back — regardless of the worst-of. This partially neutralises the worst-of risk. Even if Barclays collapses, if Nvidia is +5%, the investor is made whole. The investor gives up a small amount of coupon to purchase this safety valve, but the headline rate remains high because the one-star option is cheap to structure when at least one stock has high upside probability.
Investor
Higher yield than single-stock note. One Star feature means a diversified basket still has a rescue mechanism. Best for investors with a view that at least one quality stock will hold.
Issuer
Sells a basket of short puts + buys one One Star call. Net cost is moderate since the One Star call (long position) is cheap when any one stock has a meaningful chance of being positive.
Broker/Distributor
Allows offering sector-diversified baskets with high yields. The "one star" narrative — "if any one of these three quality names holds up, you get capital back" — is a compelling pitch for conviction-driven investors.
☽ Potentially Sharia-Compliant Structure
The higher coupon on a worst-of basket note reflects the premium received for selling a worst-of put option across the basket. The investor is compensated for bearing the risk that the weakest stock in the basket falls below the barrier. This is option premium income (risk-transfer compensation), not interest.

The correlation discount — the reason worst-of pays more than single-underlying notes — is also a legitimate risk premium: the investor accepts greater probability of loss in exchange for higher compensation. Both the base premium and the correlation premium are risk-linked, not interest-based.

Twin Win
Profiting from both directions — the absolute return structure
Core Mechanic
Bull market AND moderate bear market both win
The Twin Win converts negative performance into positive performance — up to the capital barrier. If the S&P 500 falls 15% at maturity but stays above the 80% barrier, the investor receives 100% + 15% (the absolute value of the drop). If it rises 20%, they receive 100% + 20%. Only if it falls below the 80% barrier does the investor lose capital.

The options engineering behind it
Long call + long down-and-out put
Structurally, the Twin Win is: a zero coupon bond (for capital protection) + a call option (upside participation) + a put option that pays off if the index falls moderately (but is knocked out if it falls too much). The "knock-out" put is cheap to buy because it extinguishes in the worst scenario, allowing the structure to be self-financing. The upside cap (+25%) is the price of buying that put.

When to recommend Twin Win
Uncertain markets, tactical view
Twin Win is ideal for range-bound or modestly volatile market environments where a client is unsure of direction. In 2022-style correction (S&P −20%), a Twin Win holder would have made money while long-only holders lost. It's a compelling alternative to cash or bonds for clients who want market-linked returns but fear a moderate drawdown. It does not work in a crash (2008) or a straight bull run beyond the cap.

Twin Win generates its absolute-return feature entirely through an option combination — a long at-the-money call, a long put (to convert downside to upside), and a sold call at the cap level. ⚠ Not Sharia-Compliant in Standard Form

Capital protected notes fund their 100% floor using a zero-coupon bond — an interest-bearing instrument. The investor's capital is split: most buys a zero-coupon bond that grows to par at maturity via a fixed interest rate, and the remainder buys a call option. The zero-coupon bond is fundamentally a fixed-interest loan at a discount, which constitutes riba.

A Sharia-compliant equivalent would replace the zero-coupon bond with a Murabaha structure or Sukuk (Islamic bond) — instruments that provide capital return through trade or asset-backed mechanisms rather than fixed interest. Some Islamic banks offer Sharia-compliant capital-protected products using these substitutes, but they require independent Sharia board certification.
Credit-Linked Notes
Higher yield in exchange for credit exposure — range accruals, iTraxx baskets, and corporate single-name CLNs
What is a Credit-Linked Note?
Bond + Credit Default Swap = CLN
A Credit-Linked Note (CLN) embeds a credit default swap (CDS) into a bond. The investor lends money to the issuer AND simultaneously sells protection on a reference entity (or basket). In exchange for accepting this additional credit risk, the investor earns a significantly higher coupon than a plain bond from the same issuer.

Key risk: if a credit event (default, restructuring, failure-to-pay) occurs on the reference entity, the investor receives the market recovery value defined by ISDA rather than 100% of capital.
Product 1: Range Accrual CLN
Two risks, one note: rate range + credit
This structure pays a variable coupon based on the number of days the 10Y US Treasury CMT rate stays within a specified range — PLUS the investor accepts credit risk on a reference entity. Annual issuer call option. 5Y maturity. Full capital at risk.

Formula: Coupon = X% × (days in range / total days)

⚠ Not Sharia-Compliant in Standard Form

Credit-Linked Notes embed a Credit Default Swap (CDS), which most Sharia scholars consider non-compliant for two reasons: (1) it involves selling protection on debt instruments (conventional bonds), which are themselves interest-bearing; and (2) the CDS is considered by many scholars to be a form of speculation (maysir/gharar) on creditworthiness rather than a legitimate risk-transfer mechanism backed by real assets.

The coupon on a CLN is also partly funded by the bond's underlying interest rate — making it doubly problematic from a Sharia perspective. There is no widely accepted Sharia-compliant equivalent of a standard iTraxx CLN.

Actively Managed Certificates (AMCs)
Securitised portfolio management — the flexible alternative to a fund, available in 2–4 weeks
What is an AMC?
A tradeable certificate that tracks an actively managed strategy
An Actively Managed Certificate (AMC) is a financial instrument that allows an Index Sponsor (asset manager, family office, wealth manager) to wrap an investment strategy into a securitised, ISIN-coded product. The AMC issuer executes trades and rebalancings based on the sponsor's instructions, without requiring the sponsor to set up a full fund.

AMCs are cost-efficient (saving custodian bank trading fees), quick to market (2–4 weeks for first issuance), and flexible (any asset class from bonds to derivatives). They are traded as securities with real-time Bloomberg prices.

◈ Sharia Compliance Depends on the Underlying Strategy
An AMC is a wrapper — its Sharia compliance is determined entirely by the strategy it contains, not the AMC structure itself. The SPV/certificate structure involves no interest payments; returns are purely from the strategy's performance.

An AMC may be Sharia-compliant if:
• The strategy invests only in halal assets (equities screened for prohibited sectors, Sukuk, Sharia-compliant commodities)
• The strategy avoids leverage through interest-bearing borrowing
• The Index Sponsor (strategy manager) follows AAOIFI or other recognised Sharia screening standards
• An independent Sharia supervisory board has reviewed and approved the strategy methodology

Putting it together: building a product
The options budget determines what you can afford
Every feature has a cost in the options budget (the yield from STRC or the bank bond). Your job as a structurer is to allocate that budget optimally:

• Autocall: earns budget (you sell a call to the issuer)
• Memory coupon: spends budget (you buy path-dependent protection)
• Worst-of: earns budget (you sell correlation)
• Lower barrier: earns budget (you sell more put)
• One Star: spends budget (you buy an escape call)
• Capital floor: spends budget (you buy a put)

A STRC-powered structure replaces the bank bond yield with an 11.25% engine, giving ~USD 14 of options budget per USD 100. That budget is then spent and earned across the combination of features above.
The broker's sweet spot
Sell the narrative, not the formula
Each feature exists because it solves a client objection:

"I'm worried markets go down" → Barrier + One Star
"I need income every quarter" → Guaranteed coupon RC
"I hate losing coupons" → Memory feature
"Markets might go either way" → Twin Win
"I want BTC but can't afford to lose" → 90% Floor + 95% participation
"I want high yield but diversified" → Worst-of Phoenix

The structurer's job is to map client fears and desires to the correct combination of bells and whistles — then price it so the yield is compelling enough to justify the risk.
